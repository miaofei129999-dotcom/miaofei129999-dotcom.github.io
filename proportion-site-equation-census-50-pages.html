A Census of One Calculator Site: 50 Pages, 30 Equations, 14 Ideas

Fifty pages. Thirty worked equations. Fourteen distinct equations. One of them used thirteen times.

That is the whole teaching surface of a site that gets built to answer questions about proportions, and it is the number I did not want.

Everything below comes from a single crawl, not from opinions about our own writing.

Method

sitemap.xml gave fifty URLs. Sequential curl through a proxy, 300 ms between requests, because the server behind this shares a 1 vCPU box with seventeen other sites and does not enjoy company.

Four requests timed out first time. Four answered 200 on the next try. So: fifty live pages, four slow starts, zero dead links. If we had trusted the first pass we would now be maintaining a phantom outage report.

Text was extracted by stripping <script>, <style> and tags. Equations by regex on a/b = x/c. Similarity by five-word shingles and Jaccard. No browser involved, which matters later.

Three findings

The duplicate-page worry was wasted effort. Six posts with near-identical titles about solving for x in a fraction. 528 pairwise comparisons, highest Jaccard 5.5%, nothing above 30%. They are separate articles. The overlap problem is not text, it is that all roads lead to 3/4 = x/20.

Length is not the missing thing. Pages with no equation: 1,068 words on average. Pages with one: 1,115. Four percent. The prose exists at full length; the arithmetic was simply never added.

Concentration beats coverage. Our decimals page breaks the pattern with six different equations, and it is the only page that reads like someone sat down with the numbers. Same template. Different care.

What this crawl cannot see

The calculator itself. Every page ships no type="number" field and one <svg>; the tool is built in JavaScript after load, so a text crawl gets a shell. Fraction input behaviour, rounding, the mobile layout of the steps panel — all untested here. This is an audit of prose only.

And a correction we owe ourselves: our first automated check flagged six wrong answers on our own pages. Every one was the regex reading an intermediate step as a final result. We nearly published it. The checker was the broken thing.
